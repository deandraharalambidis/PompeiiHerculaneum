Survey & Context

Pompeii and Herculaneum were Roman cities in the region of Campania — known to Romans as Campania Felix ("fortunate Campania"). The fertile volcanic soil, mild climate and sheltered Bay of Naples made the region one of the most prosperous in the Roman Empire. The area supported wine, olive oil, grain and garum production, and attracted wealthy Romans as a holiday destination. The key to understanding the sites lies in recognising that their destruction was also their preservation.

The Eruption of AD 79

Mount Vesuvius erupted in a catastrophic multi-phase eruption. After an initial pumice fall (Plinian phase), a series of pyroclastic density currents (PDCs) buried both cities. Herculaneum was buried up to 23 metres deep; Pompeii under 4–5 metres. Vulcanologist Haraldur Sigurdsson reconstructed the eruption sequence by studying grain sizes in volcanic strata and comparing with the 1980 Mt St Helens eruption and Pliny the Younger's eyewitness account. The traditional date of 24 August has been challenged — a graffito found in 2018 in Regio V suggests 17 October (or late October) as the true date.

Discovery & Excavation

Pompeii was accidentally discovered in 1592 when workmen digging a canal found marble slabs and frescoed walls. Herculaneum was discovered in 1709 by Austrian Count d'Elbeuf. Systematic excavation began in 1748 under de Alcubierre — initially treasure-hunting in style. Giuseppe Fiorelli (1860) revolutionised the field by introducing systematic region-numbering, top-down excavation and the iconic plaster cast technique. Subsequent directors Spinazzola (1910–23) and Maiuri (1923–61) continued the work, though the latter's methods were sometimes controversial. Today the Great Pompeii Project (2012–) leads conservation-focused work.

Focus of Study — Evidence & Society

The HSC Focus of Study examines a wide range of evidence: site layout, streetscapes, public and private buildings, inscriptions, graffiti, wall paintings, mosaics, statues, human and animal remains. This evidence illuminates the economy (forum, trade, garum, bakeries), social structure (elite, freedmen, slaves), local politics (duumviri, aediles, comitium), everyday life (housing, baths, food, leisure), religion (household gods, imperial cult, mystery cults) and the influence of Greek and Egyptian cultures.

Reconstructing & Conserving the Past

New technologies — CT scanning, GIS mapping, 3D modelling, X-ray phase-contrast tomography and isotope analysis — continue to transform our understanding of the sites. Major conservation challenges include water damage, plant growth, animal activity, poor drainage, and mass tourism (3+ million visitors per year). A 2011 UNESCO/ICOMOS report identified serious conservation failures. International contributions include the Packard Humanities Institute at Herculaneum and the EU's funding of conservation works. Ethical debates surround the excavation and display of human remains.

Geographic context — Campania & the Bay of Naples

The setting of Pompeii and Herculaneum was central to their prosperity — and their destruction

🌋 Mount Vesuvius

A somma stratovolcano dominating the Campanian plain. Its rich volcanic soil — grey-black and rust-brown, high in phosphorus and potash — made the surrounding land exceptionally fertile. The last eruption before 79 AD had been 800 years earlier, so residents had no knowledge they lived near a volcano. Modern scientists monitor it with seismometers, GPS and gas analysis.

🌊 Bay of Naples

The sheltered bay provided anchorages for trading vessels and the Roman navy. Tacitus called it "a most beautiful bay — before the eruption changed the landscape." Wine, wheat, oil and garum were exported across the Mediterranean. The bay also attracted wealthy Romans as a holiday destination — emperors Nero and Augustus both owned villas in the region.

🌾 Campanian Plain

Romans called the region Campania Felix — "fortunate Campania." Hot dry summers and mild wet winters supported wheat, barley, peaches, figs, cabbages and onions. Vines were especially important: Pliny wrote that in Campania the vines "never stop growing," producing the famous Vesuviana wine. Grapevines, olives and sheep were the economic backbone.

🏙 Pompeii vs Herculaneum

Pompeii: larger city (~20,000 pop.), 3.2km defensive wall, seven gates, grid street system, strong commercial character. Herculaneum: smaller (~4,000–5,000 pop.), wealthier residential character, 7km from Vesuvius peak. Both show clear Roman urban planning with forum, baths, theatres and temples, but each city experienced the eruption differently due to wind direction.

🐟 Coastline & Marine Resources

The coastline provided rich marine resources: fish and shellfish that fuelled the garum industry. Florus described Campania's coastline as "the finest, not only in Italy, but in the entire world." The port of Pompeii was less than 1km from the city centre, with 20 identified warehouses. The port made Pompeii a key node in Mediterranean trade.

🪨 Local Materials

Volcanic materials shaped the built environment: tufa and lava used for building; pumice stone for tools; volcanic sand mixed with lime for cement. Local clay enabled pottery production. Millstones made from basalt lava ground grain — Cato noted Pompeii's volcanic stone olive presses were the finest available. The volcano was as much a resource as a threat.

The Eruption of AD 79 — phases and impact

Phases of the eruption (Sigurdsson's reconstruction)

Phase 1 — Plinian Column

Weak explosions followed by a massive eruption column 14–27km high. White pumice fell over Pompeii (wind direction spared Herculaneum). A 1-metre-thick layer covered Pompeii during this phase. Pliny described the cloud as resembling a pine tree — now called a "Plinian" eruption in his honour.

⚠ Pumice falls on Pompeii; Herculaneum relatively unaffected

Phase 2 — First Pyroclastic Surges (PDCs)

The column partially collapsed, producing pyroclastic density currents (PDCs) at 330–500°C. Herculaneum was hit with a 1.5-metre layer of material. A third weak PDC reached 200 metres into Pompeii. At Herculaneum, those on the beach in boat sheds died within two minutes — temperatures of over 400°C caused fulminant shock.

✕ Fatal for Herculaneum's remaining population

Phase 3 — Re-established Column & Further PDCs

A second column reached 32km. A second PDC left 7m of material over Herculaneum. Further surges hit Pompeii; people caught outside died of asphyxiation as spongy ash formed a paste in their lungs. One-third of Pompeii's victims were found outside buildings on top of pumice, suggesting they tried to escape but were caught in subsequent PDCs.

✕ Mass casualties at both sites

Phase 4 — Final Violent Surges

The volcanic structure began to weaken. The most powerful PDC covered Pompeii at ~300km/h. Violent earthquakes, a tsunami-like sea withdrawal, and at least six total PDCs buried Pompeii under 2–5m and Herculaneum under up to 23m. Eight new volcanic ash layers have been found above Pompeii in recent excavations.

✕ Cities completely buried; sealed for 1,700 years

Impact on Pompeii

Buried 4–5m deep. Most second storeys destroyed; ground floors preserved by pumice. 1,214 bodies found — one-third outside buildings. Many in the 'boxer posture' caused by extreme heat contracting muscles. At least 600 died when roofs collapsed under pumice weight. The absence of horses and pack animals suggests many people fled early.

Impact on Herculaneum

Buried up to 23m deep. Many second storeys survived — PDCs were less destructive than pumice falls for upper floors. 349 bodies found — most on the beach or in boat sheds. Flesh disappeared immediately, leaving skeletons with cracked skulls from thermal shock. The dense volcanic material (lar) dried like concrete, making excavation extremely difficult.

The Date Debate

Traditional date: 24–25 August 79 AD (based on Pliny's letters).

New evidence: Autumn fruits, braziers for warmth, a coin minted after September 79 AD, and — crucially — a charcoal graffito found in 2018 reading "the 16th day before the calends of November" (= 17 October) all point to a late October eruption.

HSC use: Demonstrates how new evidence changes our understanding; shows the limits of ancient literary sources.

Why didn't people know the danger?

01

No living memory of eruption

The last eruption was ~800 years before 79 AD — no living memory, no written record available to residents

02

Images showed a single-peaked mountain

Frescoes show Vesuvius with one peak — the second was formed by the 79 AD eruption. Residents didn't recognise it as volcanic

03

Earthquakes not connected to volcanic activity

Hurley: no scientific knowledge existed to link tremors to volcanic eruption; 62 AD earthquake seen as isolated event

04

Fertile soil masked the threat

Volcanic soil created the region's prosperity — residents valued the mountain's productivity and had no reason to fear it

Representations over time

18th Century — Grand Tour

It became fashionable to paint dining rooms in "Pompeian red." Wealthy young men on the Grand Tour visited the sites. Skeletons were staged dramatically for visitors rather than left in situ. The day of Pompeii's destruction was portrayed as God's revenge on a pagan society.

19th Century — Romanticism

Pompeii inspired neo-classical and Romantic movements. Edward Bulwer-Lytton's 1834 novel The Last Days of Pompeii — based on skeletons he observed — had lasting influence. He kept a Pompeian skull on his desk for inspiration. The novel has been adapted repeatedly for film, opera and theatre (including the 2014 film Pompeii).

20th Century — Documentary Age

Modern photography, documentaries and the internet provided wider, more accurate images. The BBC's 2003 documentary Pompeii reached global audiences. Robert Harris's 2003 novel Pompeii attempted to balance authenticity and accessibility. Harris noted the difficulty: "if it's accessible it's not authentic and if it's authentic it's not accessible."

21st Century — World Heritage

UNESCO World Heritage listing and new media have driven renewed interest. The Great Pompeii Project's discoveries receive global coverage. Social media means individual finds — like the 2018 charcoal graffito or the ceremonial chariot — reach mass audiences instantly, shaping public understanding of the sites.

Changing Interpretations

HSC syllabus dot-point: Impact of new research and technologies on our understanding of Pompeii and Herculaneum

How interpretations have changed over time

18th–19th Century — Treasure Hunting Era

Early excavations under de Alcubierre (1748) were conducted as treasure-hunting operations for the royal Bourbon collection. Only objects considered suitable for royal display were removed; many others were destroyed in the process. No records were kept of find-spots or context. Interpretation was purely aesthetic — objects were viewed as art, not historical evidence. Johann Winckelmann's critical account was the first to demand more systematic approaches.

Fiorelli's Revolution — 1860s

Fiorelli transformed interpretation by treating the site scientifically. His plaster cast technique revealed the human cost of the eruption for the first time. His regional numbering system allowed objects to be located precisely within the city. His top-down, house-by-house excavation method preserved context. Later scholars, including Maiuri, built on his methods while sometimes compromising them under political and funding pressures.

20th Century — Decline vs. Prosperity Debate

Maiuri (1923–61) argued both cities were in economic and social decline before the eruption, damaged by the 62 AD earthquake. This interpretation shaped scholarship for decades. More recent economic historians, particularly Jongman, have challenged this view, arguing the cities showed signs of economic vitality rather than decline. The debate demonstrates how interpretations shift as new methods and evidence emerge.

21st Century — Conservation and Context

Since the 1980s, the emphasis has shifted from excavation and display to conservation, documentation and contextual analysis. New interpretations focus on the social history of ordinary people rather than elite culture alone. Lazer's bioarchaeological work challenged previous claims about health and lifespan. The 2018 graffito that revised the eruption date demonstrated that even the most basic facts remain open to reinterpretation.

Representations of Pompeii and Herculaneum over time

17th–18th Century — Grand Tour and Neoclassicism

The sites were first represented through the lens of neoclassical aesthetics. Objects were collected, displayed and painted as examples of Greco-Roman artistic achievement. Pompeian decorative styles — Pompeian red walls, geometric floor mosaics — became fashionable across Europe. Visitors on the Grand Tour sought authentic experiences of antiquity, and Pompeii provided them. Skeletons were arranged dramatically for visitors rather than left in situ. The day of destruction was often depicted as divine punishment for Pompeii's pagan vices.

19th Century — Romanticism and Popular Culture

Edward Bulwer-Lytton's 1834 novel The Last Days of Pompeii established the enduring popular image of the disaster. He based characters on skeletons he observed, gave them names and relationships, and climaxed the story with the eruption. The novel was adapted repeatedly for opera, theatre, film and television. It established Pompeii as a story about individual human tragedy rather than historical archaeology — a framing that still dominates popular culture (including the 2014 film). Bulwer-Lytton kept a Pompeian skull on his desk as inspiration.

20th Century — Scientific and Documentary

Photography, film and television transformed how Pompeii was represented. The BBC's 2003 documentary used new scientific evidence and realistic reconstruction to present a more accurate picture than the Romantic tradition. Robert Harris's 2003 novel attempted to balance authenticity with accessibility. Modern photographic records, documentaries and the internet began providing far wider and more accurate images of the sites — though they could not fully escape the dramatic Bulwer-Lytton tradition of focusing on the final moments.

21st Century — Digital and Social Media

UNESCO World Heritage listing and social media have democratised access to images and information about the sites. Individual discoveries — the 2018 date graffito, the Regio V ceremonial chariot, the thermopolium — reach global audiences within hours via social media. Documentaries can now use drone footage, CT scanning and digital reconstruction to provide perspectives previously impossible. However, social media also amplifies dramatic misrepresentations — and Bulwer-Lytton's tradition of storytelling persists in the 2014 blockbuster film and numerous television series.

Issues of Conservation and Reconstruction

HSC syllabus dot-point: Italian and international contributions and responsibilities

Major conservation challenges (2011 UNESCO Report)

💧

Water Damage

Lack of efficient drainage allows water infiltration that degrades both building structures and decorative elements (frescoes, mosaics). Identified as the primary structural threat by UNESCO/ICOMOS 2011.

🌿

Plant Growth

Rampant ivy and other vegetation destabilise walls and damage structures. UNESCO specifically cited ivy as a concern at Pompeii. Removal is labour-intensive and ongoing.

🐦

Animal Activity

Pigeon droppings (acidic), vermin, stray cats and dogs all contribute to ongoing degradation. Animals nest in wall cavities, dislodging masonry.

👷

Lack of Qualified Staff

Of ~1,000 staff at the Superintendent's office in 2011, only 12 were archaeologists and 3 were architects. The rest were custodial and administrative. Restoration was outsourced without quality assessment.

🏚

Inappropriate Restoration

Use of inappropriate materials and methods in past restoration work — including non-reversible concrete fills — has sometimes made buildings more rather than less vulnerable. The 2010 collapse of the House of the Gladiators exposed these failures.

🔒

Site Security

Poor security has allowed looting, vandalism, and graffiti on ancient walls. Large areas are closed due to a lack of custodians, concentrating visitor impact on a small number of accessible sites.

International contributions

The Great Pompeii Project (2012–)

Launched after the 2010 collapse of the House of the Gladiators. Brought significant new investment and international attention. New finds include a fast-food thermopolium with food intact, a ceremonial chariot, new victims in Regio V, and the October graffito revising the eruption date. A conservation-first approach guides all work.

Packard Humanities Institute

American philanthropic organisation that has funded and led conservation work at Herculaneum. Wallace-Hadrill describes "a decade's heroic work" that has addressed many conservation problems at the main Herculaneum site, including water drainage, structural stabilisation and documentation of the existing site.

European Community Funding

In 1984, following the 1980 earthquake, the European Community contributed 36 billion lire (approximately AUD$70 million) for restoration of Pompeii. French archaeologist Jean-Pierre Adam was employed in 1983 to evaluate restoration methods. This marked a shift from excavation to conservation as the primary goal.

UNESCO World Heritage Status

World Heritage listing brought international scrutiny as well as prestige. The 2011 UNESCO/ICOMOS report publicly documented conservation failures at Pompeii and Herculaneum, applying pressure that accelerated the establishment of the Great Pompeii Project. Listing both protects and publicises the sites.

Scholarly perspectives on key ethical debates

Lazer — Dignity for the Dead

"The attribution of names to any of Vesuvius' victims is very doubtful since no one has their name engraved on their bones... the bones should be allowed to speak for themselves."

Lazer's position is that the interpretive tradition — begun with Fiorelli and continued in documentaries — of naming victims and assigning them stories is both unreliable and disrespectful. She argues that modern scientific analysis (isotope analysis, DNA, CT scanning) can tell us genuinely new things about victims without the need for speculative narrative.

Kenny vs. Wallace-Hadrill — Villa of the Papyri

"Posterity will not forgive us if we squander this chance." — Kenny; "The logic that drives any modern excavation must be preservation, not the pursuit of a dream." — Wallace-Hadrill

These two positions represent a fundamental divide in archaeological ethics: the obligation to recover knowledge versus the obligation to preserve what already exists. Both scholars accept that the scrolls are irreplaceable. They disagree on which risk — loss through volcanic disruption, or loss through inadequate conservation of newly excavated material — is greater.

Beard — Post-Eruption Complexity

"House tunnelled — perhaps a message to other members of their gang, the ancient equivalent of 'we have already been here.'"

Beard's identification of post-eruption activity at Pompeii adds an uncomfortable dimension to the ethics of identification. If some skeletons are not eruption victims, then all individual identifications become suspect. Her argument does not undermine the ethical case for treating remains with dignity — it strengthens it, by demonstrating how little we can reliably know about any individual victim.

The Code of Ethics for Museums

"Human remains, and materials of sacred significance, should be acquired only if they can be housed and cared for respectfully."

The international Code of Ethics for Museums provides the formal framework within which decisions about human remains must be made. It requires that remains be treated with dignity, housed appropriately, and managed with respect for cultural sensitivities. Most museums now follow these guidelines, though their application to the specific context of Pompeii's plaster casts — which are simultaneously scientific specimens, tourist attractions and the mortal remains of individual people — remains contested.

Historical context — from Grand Tour to mass tourism

18th Century — The Grand Tour

It became customary for wealthy young men to visit Pompeii and Herculaneum as part of the 'Grand Tour' — a rite of passage marking their education and social status. Visits were leisurely and scholarly. Skeletons were staged dramatically for visitors rather than left in situ. The sites were portrayed as God's punishment of a pagan society. Visiting became fashionable across Europe.

19th Century — Romantic Tourism

Pompeii became a must-see destination for wealthy travellers, artists and writers. Edward Bulwer-Lytton's 1834 novel The Last Days of Pompeii drove enormous public interest. Fascination with the macabre — particularly the human remains — shaped what visitors sought to experience. Artists, poets and intellectuals including Goethe, Shelley and Mark Twain all visited and wrote about the sites.

20th Century — Mass Tourism Begins

After WWII, rising living standards and cheaper travel transformed Pompeii from an elite destination into a mass-tourism site. Visitor numbers climbed steadily. The nature of tourism shifted — from extended scholarly visits to rapid, checklist-driven tours. The infrastructure of the site (pathways, signage, custodial supervision) struggled to keep pace with visitor growth. By the late 20th century Pompeii was under serious pressure.

21st Century — Cruise Ship Tourism

Cruise ship tourism transformed the visitor profile at Pompeii. Passengers arrive in Naples, join a set itinerary (Mt Vesuvius and Pompeii day trip; Pompeii half-day from Naples; Naples and Pompeii combined tour) and have only a few hours on site. They follow the same 'must-see' routes in large groups, concentrating impact on a small number of locations. Herculaneum and other Vesuvian sites receive far fewer cruise visitors. UNESCO noted 300,000 visitors in a single peak month in 2010.

✅ Positive impacts of tourism

💰

Funding for Conservation

Economic benefit

Ticket revenue from Pompeii's 3M+ annual visitors provides significant income used to fund conservation, staffing, and the Great Pompeii Project. Without visitor income, funding for ongoing maintenance would be severely reduced. UNESCO World Heritage listing — itself driven by tourism pressure — brought international conservation funding.

🌍

Global Awareness

Cultural value

Tourism drives global public awareness of the sites' significance and vulnerability. Media coverage of tourist visits — including documentaries, social media posts and travel writing — shapes public understanding of Roman history and the ongoing conservation crisis. This awareness creates political will for investment in preservation.

🏙

Local Economy

Economic benefit

Tourism supports the wider Campanian economy — hotels, restaurants, transport operators and souvenir vendors all depend on the flow of visitors. The region's identity as a tourist destination is inseparable from the sites. Decline in visitor numbers (as during COVID-19) caused significant economic hardship for local communities.

📖

Educational Value

Cultural benefit

Direct encounter with the ruins — streetscapes, plaster casts, frescoes — provides an educational experience unavailable through books or documentaries. For students and scholars, on-site visits remain irreplaceable. The sites' accessibility to the public is a key argument against radical preservation measures (such as closing large sections) that would protect structures but end public engagement.

⚠ Negative impacts of tourism

01

Foot traffic and physical erosion

2,000-year-old paving worn by millions of annual footsteps; stepping stones and path edges deteriorating; accessible areas disproportionately impacted

02

Humidity and heat from crowds

Body heat and moisture in enclosed spaces degrade frescoes; the lupanar (brothel) cited as a specific example; UNESCO 2011 flagged humidity as key factor

03

Vandalism, graffiti and souvenir theft

Modern graffiti on ancient walls; tourists removing fragments; bags rubbing frescoes; poor supervision and signage; Alia Wallace on intentional and unintentional damage

04

Infrastructure pressure and development

Car parks, restaurants and facilities around the site; UNESCO 2011 flagged uncontrolled surrounding development; irreversible changes to the landscape

05

Cruise tourism — concentrated, brief visits

Cruise passengers on timed excursions crowd must-see routes; less visited areas receive no benefit; money spent on ships not locally; hastens decay at specific locations

06

Insufficient supervision and access control

UNESCO 2011: only 12 archaeologists among 1,000 staff; accessible areas overcrowded; closed areas entered by tourists; inadequate signage

🔧 Proposed and implemented solutions

Visitor Quotas and Timed Entry

Limiting the number of visitors at any one time, particularly in sensitive enclosed spaces. Timed entry tickets spread visitor flow across the day rather than concentrating it at peak times. Several scholars and the 2011 UNESCO report recommended formal visitor management plans that had not yet been implemented at the time.

New Pathways and Visitor Routes

Rispoli and Siannelli proposed new visitor pathways that spread tourists across more of the site, reducing pressure on the most popular locations. Opening previously closed sections — made possible by the Great Pompeii Project's conservation work — would distribute visitor impact more evenly and reduce overcrowding at the most fragile sites.

Improved Signage and Education

Better multilingual signage explaining the fragility of specific locations and why barriers must be respected. Educational materials provided before or during visits to encourage responsible behaviour. Digital guides and augmented reality tools allow visitors to engage deeply with the site without touching or entering fragile structures.

The Great Pompeii Project

Launched in 2012, the Project has both conserved existing structures and opened new areas to visitors — helping to spread tourism impact. Drone mapping, 3D modelling and new digital infrastructure have improved site management. The Project has made Pompeii more resilient to tourism pressure while simultaneously increasing its appeal as a destination.

Historian debate — the value of tourism at Pompeii

Mary Beard — Tourism is Overstated as a Threat

"It doesn't really matter if tourists damage Pompeii."

Beard argues that Pompeii has never been a pristine, intact time-capsule. The site was looted within weeks of the eruption, damaged by Allied bombing in WWII, poorly excavated for centuries, and subjected to decades of inadequate conservation. Framing tourist behaviour as the primary threat misrepresents the site's history of damage. She argues that public engagement and accessibility are more important than perfect preservation.

UNESCO/ICOMOS — Tourism is a Serious Threat

"Accessible parts are over-visited and suffer considerably from visitor erosion." (2011 Report)

The 2011 UNESCO/ICOMOS report directly linked tourist pressure to structural deterioration. It noted that 2.3 million visitors in 2010, concentrated in accessible areas, caused measurable erosion. The report called for a formal visitor management plan, improved supervision and investment in opening new areas to spread the impact. Tourism was identified as both an economic necessity and a conservation crisis.

Alia Wallace — Behaviour, Not Just Numbers

"Much of the decay comes from overcrowding and damaging behaviours, both intentional and unintentional."

Wallace's archaeological perspective focuses on specific visitor behaviours rather than visitor numbers alone. Even respectful tourists cause damage through the cumulative effect of body heat, moisture and physical contact. Bags rubbing against frescoes in crowded corridors, leaning on walls and foot traffic on fragile paving are cited as key mechanisms of damage. Her argument supports targeted solutions — better pathways, improved supervision — over blanket visitor restrictions.

Robert Harris — The Accessibility Dilemma

"You tread such a narrow line between trying to make it authentic and make it accessible." (On writing about Pompeii, 2003)

Harris's reflection on writing his 2003 novel captures a broader dilemma: the more accessible a site (or its story) is made, the more it attracts visitors — increasing the very pressure it is trying to manage. Full authenticity (restricted access, minimal reconstruction) reduces visitor appeal; maximum accessibility increases damage. This tension has no neat resolution and defines the ongoing debate about Pompeii's future.
